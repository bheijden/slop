I do battery development. What people here consistently get wrong is assuming the venture model is a neutral container any technology can be poured into. It is a machine tuned very precisely to one shape of problem, and batteries are close to the exact inverse of that shape.

Start with what makes the cycle work in software: three people and eight weeks produce something that runs. It may be ugly and it may not scale, but an investor can click on it, and that artifact is what lets money move in small increments against visible progress. A battery has no equivalent. Every interesting claim is a claim about behaviour over hundreds or thousands of cycles at temperature, and you cannot compress that timeline with money or cleverness. Years pass before there is anything a non-specialist can evaluate, and in the meantime you have coin cells and plots that look identical whether or not you are onto something.

Then there is who does the work. Software startups run on people who will trade salary and hours for equity, and in that labour pool the trade is culturally normal. The people who can actually do electrochemistry are PhD scientists and process engineers who spent a decade in national labs or corporate R&D, are frequently in their late thirties with families, and reasonably expect a salary, benefits, and a workday that ends. You will not sprint them, you will not find many of them, and you are bidding against employers who can pay cash indefinitely and will still be there in fifteen years.

The outcome distribution is the deeper problem. This field is nearly binary. Almost every plausible battery venture ends the same way: a portfolio of patents on processes that never became practical, sold for scrap into someone's defensive pile. One in some large number takes a share of the world's energy storage. There is no middle. Software has a graded return curve, where the mediocre outcome is an acquihire and the merely decent one is a profitable niche business, and venture math depends on that grading far more than the pitch decks admit.

Even winning is commercially awkward, because success arrives in only two shapes and both are bad. If the breakthrough is simple, say a coating, an electrolyte additive, a change in the order of two process steps, it is trivially reproduced the moment it is described, and you capture a sliver of a market you created while every incumbent with a plant ships it at scale. If it is complicated, it is usually complicated because it depends on an input that is expensive or scarce, and now your technology problem is a mining problem. Standing up an extractive industry from nothing is a capital scale venture investors do not entertain and probably should not, and the whole of it can be stranded overnight by a competing chemistry that needs none of it. You are being asked to bet a mine on the proposition that nobody finds a different answer for twenty years.

There is also very little here to disrupt. The usual thesis is that incumbents are structurally incapable of doing the thing. But fund a new battery entrant properly and you end up with a building full of salaried scientists, expensive characterisation equipment, decade-long timelines and a patent strategy, which is to say you have rebuilt the research arm of a consumer-electronics conglomerate, with worse purchasing power and no product revenue to ride out the wait.

The genuinely frustrating part is that nearly all the effort goes straight at the prize. Everyone wants to be the one who finds the cell. Almost nobody builds the instrumentation, the accelerated-ageing methods, the automated formulation and screening rigs that would let everyone else search the space ten times faster. That is a real business, with graded returns and customers who exist today, and it is exactly what the search is short of.

The short version: ordinary startup risk is financeable because losses are bounded and returns are graded. Battery research has a payoff that touches grid storage, transport, aerospace and every device anyone owns. That sounds like the ideal venture bet, and it is exactly why it is not one. Nobody can calculate the distribution, so nobody can price the round.
