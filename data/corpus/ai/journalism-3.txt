By the middle of 2014, Jordan Younger had built the kind of life the internet had recently begun to make possible for young women who photographed their lunch. Her blog, The Blonde Vegan, had tens of thousands of followers; she sold them a five-day cleanse of raw juices and raw plant foods, and thousands bought it. What her followers could not see was that the woman selling them purity was dismantling her own health. Her hair was thinning. Her periods had stopped. Her skin had taken on an orange cast, the consequence of relying on sweet potatoes as her only permitted carbohydrate, the beta-carotene accumulating faster than her body could use it. She had also developed something her audience had no vocabulary for: not a slimming disorder in the familiar sense, since she was not chasing thinness, but an obsessive fixation on the purity of what she ate. Clinicians have a term for it, orthorexia: the condition of being unable to eat anything that does not qualify as clean.

Her recovery was not a repudiation. She did not abandon plant-based eating; she widened it, food by food, reintroducing what her regime had ruled out until eating stopped functioning as a moral test. The interesting part is what happened when she said so in public: announcing that she was loosening her diet cost her thousands of followers within days. Customers demanded refunds. The messages that arrived were not disappointed but furious: she was a fraud, a sellout, and, most revealingly, she had simply lacked the discipline to do it properly. Some of them contained threats. She had committed the offence of admitting that the thing she was selling had made her ill.

There is nothing new about restrictive eating, or about people making money from it. Sylvester Graham was preaching against condiments and white bread in the 1830s; John Harvey Kellogg was treating the American bowel as a moral problem before the First World War; the twentieth century produced a fresh quack cure every decade. What made clean eating different was not its severity but its architecture. Older food fads were sold as improvements on ordinary eating. Clean eating defined itself against ordinary eating, as its opposite and its antidote, and it did so on platforms built for images, which meant it could reach a mass audience with claims stated far more absolutely than any publisher's lawyer would once have permitted. A photograph of a bowl of spiralised courgette makes an argument that no sentence has to defend.

The argument it made was the movement's real innovation. Conventional food, in this account, was not merely fattening; it was contaminating. Sugar was not a source of surplus calories but a poison; gluten and dairy were not simply things some people cannot digest but substances that clogged, inflamed and corrupted. Once food is described that way, a diet stops being a diet and becomes something closer to a doctrine. The vocabulary gives it away: clean and dirty, pure and toxic, cleansing, detoxing, sinning, being good. Adherents did not merely follow a regime, they confessed lapses and sought absolution. This was a framework of virtue with a shopping list attached, and, like other such frameworks, it treated illness as a failure of observance. That is why Younger's followers turned on her. If purity guarantees health, then a sick believer must have cheated.

The commercial effects were immediate and easy to measure. Publishers who, only a few years earlier, had turned down recipes on the grounds that readers had never heard of chia seeds, cacao nibs or coconut oil found those ingredients moving from health-food shops to supermarket aisles and then to own-brand ranges. Cookbooks by young women with large followings and no training sold in numbers career food writers had never approached. An entire retail category was built on the proposition that the ordinary contents of a British kitchen were dangerous.

The backlash, when it came, arrived from two directions. Cookery writers read the whole enterprise as body-shaming in a health-conscious disguise, and as a well-lit path into disordered eating for the young women it recruited most successfully; Ruby Tandoh made that case forcefully, and Nigella Lawson has said plainly that she loathes the phrase, on the grounds that it implies all other food is dirty. Scientists and food journalists went after the specific claims, of which coconut oil became the emblem: promoted as a metabolic marvel, it is a fat around 80 to 90 per cent saturated, and cardiologists have been consistently unenthusiastic about it.

At the far end of the spectrum the consequences were not rhetorical. Robert O Young, an American promoter of the theory that disease is caused by acidity and cured by alkalinity, ran a ranch in California where seriously ill people were treated with intravenous bicarbonate and vegetable juice. Among his patients was a young British woman with terminal cancer, who paid him tens of thousands of dollars in the months before she died. Young held no medical qualification worth the name, and was later convicted of practising medicine without a licence. He was an extreme, but he was not a departure. He simply took the movement's central premise, that the body is contaminated and can be purified by what enters it, and applied it to people who were dying.

What happened next was instructive. As the criticism accumulated, the movement's most prominent figures did not defend clean eating; they disowned the phrase. They had never used it, they said, or would not use it any more; it had been imposed on them by the media. The books, the courses, the jars and the followings continued unchanged, relabelled as wellness, as balance, as nourishment. This is the usual life cycle of a food ideology, and its persistence is not mysterious. It offers something an anxious relationship with an industrial food supply genuinely lacks: a legible rule, a visible virtue, and the promise that the part of your life you can actually control might be the part that saves you.
