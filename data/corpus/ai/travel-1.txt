We reached the little capital in the forenoon, and by two o'clock the Reverend and I had used up its public buildings, its shipping and one of its principal citizens, and had nothing left but a walk. It lasted four hours and settled between us a question of the first magnitude: what is this town made out of?

That it was made of something uncommon was plain in the first street. It was white. Not white as a painted house is white, but white all through, in a hard, glossy, unanswerable fashion that made you glad you had a hat. The Reverend said limestone. I said it was not, limestone being a sullen, gray-hearted, respectable material, and this was gay. We argued it up one street and down another with great learning on both sides and no information on either; then we walked into the country, and the country answered us.

Cut into the flank of a low hill by a gate was a hole: a rectangular pit some thirty feet by fifteen, its walls going down sheer and smooth, without a bruise or a stain on them, as clean as the inside of a bandbox. The Reverend, who has an orderly mind, said the house behind us had plainly been cast in that mould like a pudding and lifted out whole, and that it was the most sensible arrangement he had ever heard of.

It was a beautiful theory and I was sorry to spoil it. What came out of that pit was not a house but the material of one. The island is coral from the sea up, with four inches of red earth over it, spread the way a frugal woman spreads butter; so every man who owns land owns a quarry and works it himself, at the bottom of his own garden, whenever he wants a wall. He does not blast and needs no derrick. The stone is soft. He takes a chisel on a handle twelve or fifteen feet long and works it straight up and down, as a man drills a hole with a crowbar or a woman works a churn dasher, and cuts his trenches ten and even twenty feet deep, marking off his blocks below ground as coolly as a baker scores gingerbread. Then he saws them, with an ordinary handsaw, the one he would put to a plank, into bricks two feet long by one foot wide by six inches thick, stacks them loosely in the sun a month to harden, and builds. That is the entire mystery. I give the particulars because they are the only solid things in this article.

And he builds everything out of it. The roof is coral, in thin overlapping slabs that step down from the ridge like very shallow terraces; the chimneys are coral, sawn into scrolls and knobs by a man whistling; the veranda floor is coral, the path to the gate is coral, and the fence is coral, with coral capstones and a coral gatepost at either hand, the whole trimmed to graceful lines with that same saw. Then he whitewashes the lot. Not in a thin apologetic wash you can see the wall through, but thick, over roof and chimneys and house and fence alike, till the establishment is one substance and one color; and in the sun a stranger cannot look at it. He turns his head away and is humble.

We spent some distance trying to say what the color was like. The Reverend offered marble, then withdrew it himself, marble being a tame, cold, reserved white with something of the tombstone about it. I offered snow; snow grays and slumps and turns apologetic. We got it at last, and got it together: it is the icing on a cake, that hard sugary white with the faintest sheen on it, that looks good enough to break off and eat. That is the color, and there is no other.

The coating is so thick it hides every joint and seam, and the house does not look built at all; it looks carved out of one piece, with the doors and windows cut in afterward with a knife. A white marble house is a chilly, sepulchral thing; you enter it in a hushed voice and the conversation dies at the door. These are cheerful to the point of frivolity. There was one chimney with one face in the sun and the other in shadow, the sunny side blazing sugar and the shady side a soft cool blue, and I could have looked at it an hour; and when such a house springs out at you through the oleanders at a turn of the road, you say something aloud before you know you have said it.

They are everywhere, town and country, the three-room affair on its two-acre patch and the great house of the rich man, and both equally spotless. Flowers are banked about them in reckless profusion, but no vines climb them, a vine getting no grip on that hard smooth skin and giving it up. And there is no dirt anywhere; none. No smell, no standing water, no rubbish, no disorder; the meanest cottage is as trim as the best; the roads are trim, the people are trim, their clothes are trim. It is the neatest country I know of. Where the poor live in it I could not discover, and the Reverend did not know either; we left that for the politicians of a future generation to quarrel over, and walked on.

I amused myself, going along, with setting one of these country houses down, tinted window ledges, green shutters, flowers banked to the sills, in a soot-blackened European capital, or almost any American city you care to name. It would not last the week out. But it would be worth the money while it lasted.

The roads are coral too. They do not build a road here, they cut one: a few inches into the solid rock, the bed leveled, and where a hill is in the way they go down many feet and let the road run in a white trench; and the surface, coarse and porous, is the exact image of coarse white sugar. It throws the sun back into a pedestrian's eyes with such energy that he travels the whole afternoon on the verge of a sneeze and never quite arrives.

An old sea captain overtook us toward evening and walked a piece with us, and I observed that he kept edging off the road into the grass, and coming back, and edging off again, till I supposed him lame and asked what ailed him. "Well, sir," he said, "I chaws; and dern this road, a man can't spit on it."
