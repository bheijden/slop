Your liver is easy to overlook. It sits under the ribs on the right side of your body and works without prompting, filtering toxins out of your blood, making the bile that helps you digest your food, and helping to keep your blood sugar steady between meals. A small amount of fat inside liver cells is perfectly normal. Too much is not. When fat builds up, it starts to interfere with the very work the organ is supposed to be doing.

That condition is fatty liver disease, and it is more common than most people realize. It is also, for most of its course, completely silent. There is usually no pain and no outward sign, nothing that would send you to a doctor. As a result, most people who have it have no idea. When symptoms do appear, they are vague enough to be blamed on almost anything else: unusual tiredness, or a dull discomfort in the upper right side of the abdomen. That is one reason liver specialists have been pushing for more public awareness. People who have never heard of the condition do not think to ask whether they should be checked for it.

Doctors divide the disease into two broad types based on cause. The more common is nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, which many clinicians now call metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. It develops in people who drink little or no alcohol, and its causes are tangled together: other health conditions, inherited tendencies, the food a person eats, and even the mix of bacteria in the digestive tract. By current estimates it affects roughly a third of adults in the United States.

Body weight and blood sugar shift those odds sharply. Around three quarters of people carrying excess weight have some degree of fat in the liver, and among people with severe obesity the figure rises to about nine in ten. People living with type 2 diabetes are at similarly high risk.

The second type is alcohol-associated fatty liver disease. Alcohol is broken down in the liver, and in large enough amounts it behaves less like a beverage than a toxin. Heavy, sustained drinking damages liver cells directly and leaves fat behind in the process.

A newer line of research asks whether chemicals in our surroundings play a part as well. One of the earliest clues came from a study of factory workers who handled vinyl chloride, the raw material used to make PVC plastic, and who turned out to have unusually high rates of liver disease. Since then, researchers have implicated many other chemicals. Some are still found in ordinary household products; others linger for years in soil, water and food long after their use has ended. Early evidence suggests these exposures may compound the risk that comes from diet, so that the combination does more harm than either would alone.

For many people, fat in the liver stays just that and never causes trouble. In a minority, though, the disease moves forward. The fat gives way to inflammation and injury to liver cells, and if that continues, the damaged tissue is gradually replaced by scar. A scarred liver becomes stiff and knotted, and at that stage the harm is permanent. It can end in liver failure or in liver cancer. The encouraging part of the story is that the liver is unusually good at repairing itself, provided the damage is caught before scarring takes hold. That is what makes early detection worth the trouble.

Most cases are found by accident. A routine blood test ordered for some other purpose comes back showing abnormal liver enzymes, and the search begins there. Further blood work and imaging can estimate how much fat has accumulated and whether scarring has started. Confirming the inflammatory stage, however, still requires a biopsy, in which a needle is passed through the abdominal wall to remove a small piece of the liver. That carries real risks, including pain, bleeding, puncture of nearby organs and infection. It also frequently comes back negative, meaning some people accept the risk and gain nothing. For that reason, researchers are working on painless imaging techniques that can measure both fat and scarring without a needle.

No medication has yet been approved specifically to treat fatty liver disease, so management rests on changes to daily life. Weight loss is the most powerful tool available. Studies suggest that losing about 7% of body weight can resolve the inflammatory stage of the disease, and that a loss of at least 10% is generally needed before scarring begins to recede. For someone who weighs 200 pounds, that is roughly 14 pounds and 20 pounds respectively.

Alongside weight, experts point to better nutrition, more physical activity, and limiting alcohol whatever the type of disease. Given the emerging findings on chemical exposure, some researchers also suggest washing fruits and vegetables thoroughly, to reduce pesticide residue.

Medicines may eventually join that list. Vitamin E has shown benefit in some patients, and certain diabetes drugs that also produce weight loss have improved the liver in clinical trials. More studies are under way.

There is one more reason to act. People with fatty liver disease are more likely to die of heart disease than of anything happening in the liver; cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in this group. The same steps that reduce liver fat, especially losing weight and becoming more active, also lower that risk. If you have obesity or type 2 diabetes, or if a blood test has ever come back with unexplained liver results, it is worth asking your health care provider directly whether your liver should be looked at. The condition is quiet, but it is not untreatable, and the earlier it is found, the more the liver can do to heal itself.
