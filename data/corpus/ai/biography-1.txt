Everyone knows the picture, and has known it since the schoolroom. A young woman of fortune, gently bred, turns from the ballrooms of her father's houses to the beds of the sick; she goes out to a war; and in a vast, verminous military hospital above the Bosphorus she passes along the corridors at night with a lamp in her hand, while four miles of men — dying by the hundred of cholera, of gangrene, of plain neglect — kiss her shadow where it falls on the wall. It has been worked on samplers, recited by children, cast in bronze. It is also, in nearly every particular that signifies, false. The woman who did those things was neither pliant nor gentle nor, by most accounts, especially pleasant; what moved her was not tenderness of temperament but a compulsion nearer to possession than to charity, which she never ventured to call by any name but God's. She was stranger than her legend, and for that reason far more interesting.

Nothing in the upbringing accounts for her. The family belonged to that confederacy of English money in which everybody is somebody's cousin: banking fortunes, Unitarian consciences, connections by marriage with half the prosperous names of the western counties. There were two country houses — one in Hampshire for the winter, one in the Derbyshire hills for the summer — and, for the season, rooms in a good street off Piccadilly. There were winters abroad: Italy, and a great deal of Italian opera, of which she kept meticulous account, entering the singers as another girl entered her partners; then Paris, and introductions, made with parental pride, to the wittiest women in Europe. Upon such an outlay a return was expected. The return was a suitable marriage, following in due course upon the requisite quota of dinners and dances — which was what her sister was arranging, and every young woman she knew, and what nobody thought to call a career, because it was what happened.

She did not rebel. Rebellion was not in her vocabulary, which was scriptural. She never disputed that a woman should do her duty in that station of life to which it had pleased God to call her; she disputed the identification of the station. Was it certain He had called her to the drawing-room? He had called others to stranger places — Deborah out to the battle, Jael to the tent-peg, a farmer's girl out of her village and set at the head of an army, without any nice inquiry into what became her sex. Something spoke to her. She was sixteen when she set the thing down in the flat manner of a fact: God had spoken to her, and called her to His service. What the service was He did not say. He did not say for sixteen years, and she could neither identify the voice nor silence it.

The symptoms had declared themselves early and had been thought charming. Her elder sister, a thoroughly normal child, liked to pull the dolls to pieces; the younger came behind and sewed them up again. She was taken visiting in the cottages, as young ladies were, with jelly and flannel, and, unlike other young ladies, wished to know how long it had lasted and whether the poultice had answered. She sat up all night with a dying old woman in the village and was perfectly happy. She splinted an injured dog's paw with a care the paw did not require. She furnished the house in her head as a hospital, the drawing-room cleared for beds and herself over the wards; and when she considered heaven she pictured it, without embarrassment, as a place well stocked with patients for her to be useful to. All of it went into the diary, in a hand growing yearly more agitated.

And then the bell went for dinner. This is the shape of her youth, and of both its comedy and its pathos: the exaltation, and then the bell. She would rise from her knees, or from the diary, with the question of her existence beating in her — what was she for? was the voice God's, or her own vanity got up in a surplice? were thirty years to go in the arrangement of flowers? — and would dress, because one dressed; and come down to be agreeable; and afterwards see to the china, and read aloud to her father in the evening. A fortnight of her life she once summarised with terrible exactness. She had read a volume of Sybil to Mama.

Her father, who had taught her Greek because it amused him, proposed the customary remedy: what the girl wanted was a husband, and there was no difficulty about that. Her mother could not conceive what ailed her. Here was a daughter, handsome, accomplished, speaking her four languages, who might have any establishment in England she chose to glance at, and who complained — the word was her own — of wanting Occupation. Occupation! With the still-room, and the poor, and the flowers, and the letters, and quite enough to occupy anybody in a house of that size.

In the winter of her twenty-fifth year she made her request. It was modest: three months at the infirmary of a neighbouring county town, to learn nursing. Being honest, she confessed a further design — a small house in the village near by, and a few gently reared women gathered into it to live together and work among the sick; a sisterhood, Protestant, without vows and without a habit. The scheme was received as derangement. There was a scene; her sister became hysterical; her mother was angry, and then, having been angry, recovered herself, rang for tea, and went back to her needlework. Nothing whatever had happened. Her daughter went upstairs believing her life had been ended.

Two things made the wall unclimbable. The first was that a woman of independent means did not go out and make her own way; there was no way for her to make. The second was nursing. The hospital nurse of the period was popularly — and not seldom actually — a coarse, ignorant, dirty old woman, frequently drunk and occasionally violent, of a notorious looseness in her conduct, unfit to be trusted with the simplest medical task; the novelists of the day had fixed her for ever, gin-bottle on the chimney-piece. That the calling is now what it is, and the word carries what it carries, is very largely her own doing; but that lay ahead. To her family, as she observed long afterwards with the dryness of a woman who had won, it was precisely as if she had announced that she meant to go into service as a kitchen-maid.

What followed was not resignation but a slow curdling of unhappiness into something with a clinical look to it. She judged the life about her contemptible, and herself the more contemptible for being fitted to nothing better: 'I shall never do anything, and am worse than dust and nothing.' The frustration presented itself, inevitably, as sin. She saw herself arraigned, the books opened, the case gone against her; she was convinced that no creature living had so grieved the Holy Spirit; she prayed against her vanity and her hypocrisy, found the prayers themselves vain, and hypocritical, and entered that too. She dared not laugh. God might hear her laughing before she had repented.
