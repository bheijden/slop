Participants moved their beliefs in the direction of the evidence they received. Statements accompanied by a confirmation were subsequently believed more strongly than they had been at baseline, and statements accompanied by a correction were believed less strongly, with both shifts measured within participants and both appearing for statements attributed to either politician. Nothing in the data resembled a backfire effect: in no partisan subgroup did a correction leave participants more convinced of the claim it addressed than they had been beforehand. If the practical question about fact-checking is whether it can move belief in the claim under scrutiny, the answer here is unambiguous, and consistent with a now sizeable literature in which the reversals reported by early studies have failed to replicate.

More notable is what we did not observe. Motivated reasoning, in the form of attenuated updating when a correction was inconvenient for the participant's own side, was absent. If anything the pattern ran in the opposite direction: corrections were somewhat more effective when the corrected statement had been made by the politician favoured by the participant's party affiliation, and this asymmetry was carried mainly by left-leaning participants. We are cautious about over-reading an effect of this size, but its sign matters, because the directional-motivation account makes an unambiguous prediction here and the data do not deliver it.

The results for support are more qualified. A politician's support ratings, and the voting intentions that tracked them, declined only when the set of fact-checks a participant saw was heavily weighted towards corrections. Where corrections and confirmations were balanced, support was indistinguishable from that of participants who saw no fact-checks at all, even though the belief measures in those same conditions had shifted. Belief revision and attitude revision therefore came apart: participants accepted that particular claims were false without revising their evaluation of the person who had made them.

We read this decoupling as broadly consistent with the position that corrections meet resistance chiefly when accepting them would require abandoning an attitude that is both strongly held and personally important. The statements used here were real, and many of them concerned matters that, while politically salient in the abstract, are unlikely to be constitutive of anyone's political identity: figures about programme costs, claims about historical decisions, characterisations of an opponent's record. Learning that such a claim is false imposes little on a supporter, because the attitude towards the politician can absorb the loss of any single statement without strain. On this reading, the null attitudinal effect is not evidence that corrections cannot touch attitudes, but evidence that these corrections did not engage the attitudes that would have had to be defended.

That interpretation is testable, and the obvious next step is to manipulate the property we could not control here. A design that contrasts fact-checks on peripheral or obscure issues with fact-checks on issues central to a politician's platform, while measuring issue-specific attitudes alongside evaluations of the politician, would establish whether resistance appears once the corrected claim carries identity-relevant weight. It would also allow the mediating path to be examined directly, since the account predicts that centrality moderates the belief-to-attitude link rather than belief revision itself.

We also found no evidence that right-leaning participants resisted corrections more than left-leaning participants did. This is worth stating plainly, given how often asymmetry is assumed. The individual-difference traits commonly linked to political conservatism, among them dogmatism, need for cognitive closure and heightened sensitivity to threat, are not obviously implicated by the stimuli we used. A short, sourced correction, presented without adversarial framing, is not a threatening object, and there is little reason to expect closure motivation to be activated by a task that supplies rather than withholds a definite answer. Asymmetries observed elsewhere may depend on features of the correction context that our procedure removed.

One partisan difference did appear at baseline: left-leaning participants believed statements from their preferred politician more strongly than right-leaning participants believed statements from theirs. We are reluctant to interpret this. Using genuine statements from two real politicians buys ecological validity at the cost of experimental control, and the two figures differed in ways we could not equate, including the office each held at the time and their perceived credibility among our sample. A baseline difference in belief is exactly what such confounds would produce, and cannot be separated from a genuine difference in partisan credulity within this design.

Finally, the ratio effect itself does not require a partisan explanation. Participants appear to arrive with an expectation about how often a politician's statements are accurate, and a balanced set of fact-checks does little to violate it. Repeated evidence of falsehood, by contrast, accumulates into a revised impression of the source, and it is that impression, rather than any single corrected claim, that carries through to feelings about the politician and thence to reported voting intention. Consistent with this, the decline appeared irrespective of whether the politician was congruent with the participant's own party.
