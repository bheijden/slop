That in the treatise on painting left us by the greatest master of that age one living contemporary only should be named, and that the name should be Sandro Botticelli, may be no more than an accident of the pen; it may equally be the considered judgment of the single intelligence then competent to pronounce one. A hundred years ago the name was scarcely a name; it is gathering weight again, quietly, as readers who care for such things come one by one upon the charm of the work. And that recovery sets the critic the question he owes to every artist not yet quite familiar — not whether the work be good, which is soon settled and sooner forgotten, but what exactly is the sensation it excites, the particular quality of pleasure which it, and nothing else anywhere, has to give.

Place him in his sequence. By the middle of the century he had already arrived at that meditative subtlety which we are used to credit only to the great imaginative painters of its close. He had left behind him two things: the plain devotional manner which the followers of the older tradition had gone on practising, with a thinning conviction, for a hundred years; and the plain naturalism which grew out of it, and which, looked at hard, is birds and flowers only. He turned instead to what were for him modern books, to the poets of the vernacular, and to his own fresh readings of the classical fable; and when he did paint the sacred incidents he ran beneath them a sentiment of his own, private, not in the story at all, so that this undercurrent reaches you through the veil of the nominal subject as the real matter of the picture.

The life, in an age of adventurous lives, is strikingly without event. Research has thinned out the anecdote which the old biographer heaped up, dissipating the romance of Fra Filippo and clearing the fame of Andrea del Castagno; here there is not even a legend to dispel. The familiar name is not his: it is a boy's nickname fastened to the surname of the goldsmith who first taught him, his own family name being another altogether. Two things happened to him, and both he shares with other painters — a summons to Rome to paint upon the walls of the papal chapel, and, late, a submission to the influence of the reforming friar, after which he seems to drop out of sight into a religious melancholy lasting until the accepted date of his death, early in the next century. He threw himself, they say, upon the study of the great vernacular poem, and even wrote a commentary on it; still, that long inactivity sits oddly, and one half wishes some document might yet be turned up to fix the death earlier, and spare him, in our minds, that dejected old age.

For he is before all things a poetical painter, blending what is properly poetical, story and sentiment, with what is properly painting, taken abstractly, line and colour. And that is why he becomes, as if by right of nature, the illustrator of the Commedia.

Of the printed edition of 1481 one fact is worth having. At the head of each canto a blank was left for the illuminator's hand; in a few surviving copies these have been filled, as far as the nineteenth canto of the Inferno, with impressions taken from engraved plates — evidently a trial, for in one copy which lies in a university library a plate has been pulled upside down, and badly out of square, in the middle of a sumptuous printed page.

The poem had to wait for its illustrator. The earlier manner, with its almost childlike devotional aim, had not yet learned to charge outward things — light, colour, the gesture of an ordinary hand — with the burden of meaning the poem carries everywhere; before the fifteenth century no painter was in a condition to supply those images.

The designs themselves must be taken with their faults. They are crowded with incident; they blend, with an unembarrassed disregard of all pictorial decorum, three successive moments of one scene into a single plate. The monstrous figures miscarry outright, as such figures must: the poet's word presents its image faintly to the mind, and the same image, brought over into visible form, needs pitching lower than the words; one regrets that he did not rather take the milder imagery of the Purgatorio. Set against that, two things stay with you. In the punishment of those thrust head-downward in the rock there is a touch not in the text at all — a little flame catching the upturned soles of the feet — and that invented flame is proof enough that the design is a painter's own vision and not a transcript of the words. And there are the centaurs, where he forgets the circumstances of the story altogether and gives himself up with delight to the creatures themselves: small bright woodland things, with arch infant faces and dainty bodies, drawing their tiny bows.

He came of a generation of naturalists and might easily have remained one; the alertness to outward things is all through him — the lawns of that time full of delicate small creatures, its hillsides with pools upon them, its pools set about with flowering reeds. It did not satisfy him. He is a visionary painter, and in his visionariness he is of one kin with the poet he illustrated.

For there are two sorts of painters. The dramatic — the poet's own companion, and two celebrated men who came after him — transcribe the outward image with more or less refinement, and stand before the action nearly impassive spectators of it. The other sort seizes upon the material set before it as the vehicle of its own ideas, moods, visions; plays fast and loose with that material; rejects, isolates, recombines. For such a man, as for the poet, the scene, the colour, the gesture comes with a sharp and insistent reality; but by some law private to his own make it wakes in him a mood it wakes in nobody else — a mood of which the outward thing is only the double or the echo — and this he clothes again in sensuous particulars, that all the world may share it.
