It is no small thing to see a college opened in an English manufacturing town where the sciences of nature take the place that Greek and Latin take elsewhere. We are told that such a school must turn out able but narrow men, and that whatever else is altered, the classics must remain the backbone of a liberal education, because they always have been. I shall not answer that by praising my own trade, but by asking a historical question: how did the classics come by their authority, and do the conditions which gave it to them still exist?

Consider learning in western Europe between the fall of Rome and the fourteenth century. It was not an absence of intellectual life, but an intellectual life of great vigour carried on inside a closed system. The premises were furnished by theology and were not open to revision. They stood to the schoolman as axioms and postulates stand to the geometer, and his business was to reason from them, with a subtlety few of us could rival, to conclusions already known to be right. To arrive anywhere but where you were expected was not discovery but error.

The system had one great merit: it left nothing unexplained. It gave a man a universe with a beginning and an end, this earth fixed at the centre and the spheres wheeling about it for his sake. It told him that matter was base and perishable, a distraction from the spiritual business for which he existed. And it assured him that the course of nature might be set aside at any moment by spiritual agents, whose interference was not the rare exception but the standing explanation of whatever was remarkable.

The education which followed was strictly logical. If the only knowledge that signifies is that by which the soul is saved, then all serious teaching must be theological; and since the theology of the West was written, argued and administered in Latin, the road to every higher study ran through the Latin grammar. The investigation of nature, on this view, wasted a man's short time, and might be worse than a waste. He who was curious about the properties of things was suspected of trafficking with the agents supposed to govern them, and more than one patient inquirer went down to posterity as a sorcerer, which was the tribute then paid to experimental research.

The change came, as such changes do, from contact with strangers. Through Moorish Spain and the roads opened by the Crusades, Christendom met a civilisation which had kept and enlarged what the West had lost. Aristotle came back to Paris and Oxford in Arabic dress; men who had learned to read him at that remove presently wanted the originals; the Greek texts followed; and the printing press multiplied in one generation what the copyists could not have furnished in a century.

The enthusiasm which followed was justified, and the place then given to classical study rested on excellent and unsentimental grounds. Antiquity, in the fifteenth century, had no competitor. For literature, there was nothing modern fit to be named beside the Greek; for sculpture, what was dug out of the ground was better than what was being carved above it; for knowledge of the physical world, the Greek geometers, astronomers and physicians were not merely the best authorities but very nearly the only ones. And in those books a man met, for the first time, minds reasoning without knowing beforehand what they were required to conclude. That example of unforced intellectual freedom was worth more than all the grammar, and is the true debt we owe the scholars of the Revival.

Notice what happened next, for it concerns us nearly. The recovery of Greek was in its day precisely what the teaching of physical science is in ours: an innovation pressed by a minority upon a curriculum governed by men who saw no need of it, and denounced as useless and unfit for youth. The innovators won, to the lasting benefit of the world, and then treated their victory as final. The new study hardened into a discipline, the discipline into a tradition, and the tradition into a claim of right; so that the reproaches once flung at the humanists are now flung by their heirs at us.

The settlement cannot stand; not because the classics have grown less admirable, but because the world has altered under them. In the last two centuries, and above all in the last fifty years, natural knowledge has passed out of the study into daily life. It settles how we are fed, clothed, carried and cured; the prosperity of this town rests upon it; and it has quietly replaced, even in minds which never open a scientific book, the whole picture of the universe which the middle ages framed and the Renaissance left standing. We stand further from the men of the Revival than they stood from the schoolmen they despised; for their quarrel was about the sources of learning, and ours is about the nature of things.

The results of inquiry do not sit quietly beside the older account; they contradict it point by point. The earth is a minor planet of an undistinguished star; nature is of an antiquity beside which the received chronologies are nothing; matter, far from being base, exhibits the order on which all our knowledge rests; and the history of life is one of slow modification, not of interference. The credentials are of another kind also. These conclusions appeal to nature and not to authority, and any patient man with honest eyes may put them to the question. They confess themselves incomplete, and know their formulae for symbols and not for final descriptions. And they bring a rule of conduct which I commend to this college as its proper inheritance: that it is not merely mistaken, but wrong, to profess a belief which outruns the evidence for it. A man bred in that habit will not be a narrow man; and he will possess the one thing an English education cannot decently lack in this century, some real acquaintance with the order of the world he lives in.
