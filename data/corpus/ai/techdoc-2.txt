The architectural principles of the Internet have been stated before, and this document does not restate them. Its purpose is narrower: to extend that earlier statement with philosophical guidance for those who design and operate the largest backbone networks. The guidance offered here is unified by a single claim. That claim is that complexity is the principal barrier to scaling a network, and that controlling complexity is therefore how an architect controls both cost and reliability. Every other recommendation made here is a corollary.

It is worth being precise about where complexity comes from, because the intuitive answer is wrong. In most engineered systems, the machinery required to deliver the basic function is a comparatively small fraction of the whole. The bulk of the mechanism, and almost all of the mechanism that is difficult to reason about, exists to make the system robust against uncertainty: uncertainty in the environment the system will encounter, and uncertainty in the behaviour of the components from which it is built. A protocol that had to work only on a quiet link, between correct implementations, under an offered load known in advance, would be a short specification. The specifications are not short. This observation should temper the reflex that treats each additional robustness mechanism as free: robustness is what complexity is made of, and mechanisms added to cover one class of uncertainty routinely become a new source of uncertainty for someone else.

From this follows what may be called the Simplicity Principle: complexity is what impedes efficient scaling, and is consequently the largest single driver of both capital and operational expenditure in a large network. Capital expenditure follows complexity because complex designs require more capable, less commoditised, and less interchangeable equipment. Operational expenditure follows it more steeply, because complex systems demand scarce expertise, resist automation, and consume engineering attention that would otherwise go to growth. The design consequence is a discipline rather than a rule: among the workable solutions to a problem, an architect should drive the design toward the simplest, and should treat any increment of complexity as a cost to be justified rather than a capability to be celebrated.

The end-to-end argument is the most productive application of this discipline the Internet has produced. In its simplest form it holds that per-communication state belongs at the endpoints of that communication, and nowhere else. The attraction of this arrangement is not aesthetic. If the only copy of the state associated with a conversation resides at the parties to the conversation, then that state can be destroyed only by the failure of an entity that is itself a party to it, and whose failure the surviving party must in any case handle. This is the notion of fate-sharing: the state and the entity that cares about it share a fate, so no new failure mode has been introduced by keeping them together. The same reasoning produced the hourglass, whose narrow waist is a deliberately minimal IP layer. The waist is thin because everything placed in it must be implemented identically by every element in the path, and constrains what can be built above and below.

None of this means that the core of the network is stateless. The core holds a very large quantity of state, most conspicuously routing state, and it grows with the network. The important property is not the absence of state but its granularity and its independence. Routing state is coarse-grained, aggregated, and nearly orthogonal to the state held by endpoints; it describes how to reach prefixes rather than what any particular conversation is doing. Simplicity in a large network comes from minimising the interaction between these two kinds of state, not from eliminating either. This is the ground on which address translation deserves scrutiny. Whatever its operational merits, translation couples per-conversation state to a network element and reduces the transparency between network and host, and so it works against precisely the property that keeps large networks tractable.

The largest networks should be understood as a qualitatively different engineering problem from medium-sized ones. They exhibit non-linearities at the level of architecture, of design, and of engineering practice, and they are self-dissimilar: the structure observed at one scale, or at one level of abstraction, is not the structure observed at another, so intuitions calibrated on a campus or a regional network transfer poorly and misleadingly to a backbone.

Chief among these effects is what may be called the Amplification Principle: in a very large system, small perturbations at the input of a process can destabilise its output. Nature supplies the cleanest illustrations, all of them variations on resonance. A bridge is not destroyed by the average force of the wind but by a periodic excitation that couples to a mode of the structure. The gaps in the rings of a planet are not swept clean by any large event but by the repeated, minute gravitational nudges of a moon in orbital resonance. The tides, and the slow climatic cycles driven by lunar and solar periodicities, are likewise the accumulated effect of small forces applied with unfortunate regularity.

Networking supplies its own examples. As the richness of interconnection increases, routing convergence becomes both slower and more complex, so that the addition of adjacencies intended to improve resilience can degrade the behaviour of the system as a whole. A routing mesh is a process whose output is very much more complex than its input, and small changes in policy or topology at the input can produce disproportionate and unforeseen changes at the output. In a cell-switched network, the loss of a single cell destroys the entire packet of which it was a fragment, converting a negligible impairment into a substantial one. The parallel outside networking is a fly-by-wire airliner, an aircraft engineered to be robust against violent turbulence yet vulnerable to microscopic faults in a handful of processors. The mitigation in all these cases is the same: arrange the system so that the effect of a local change is confined to the local scope.
