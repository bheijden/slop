Around 2008, a veteran revenue agent working oil and gas cases noticed a gap in the ledger that nobody else seemed to be looking at. Out on the outer continental shelf, in the federal waters where American oil is drilled, much of the work is done by companies that are not American. Drilling contractors, dive outfits, helicopter crews, seismic survey vessels: many were foreign-owned and foreign-incorporated, and, under a provision of the tax code that treats the shelf as US territory for tax purposes, liable for federal tax on what they made there. Large numbers of them were not filing. Nobody was pursuing them.

The agent did what a functioning enforcement agency is supposed to allow a good employee to do. He worked out which companies were in scope, persuaded his managers to let him build a project around it, and trained other agents in the peculiar mix of international tax law and offshore industry practice the work required. Over the following years the effort recovered tens of millions of dollars that would otherwise never have been collected.

Then he retired. The project, deprived of the person who understood it, was quietly wound down. There was no announcement, no decision anyone had to defend in public. The expertise walked out of the building and the revenue stopped.

That is a small story, and it is also the whole story of what has happened to the Internal Revenue Service. Beginning in 2010, Congress cut the agency's budget year after year, a reduction of roughly a fifth in real terms after inflation. Because the IRS is not a factory and does not close plants, the cuts were absorbed the way bureaucracies absorb pain: through hiring freezes and attrition. Nobody was marched out. People retired, or left for private practice, and were simply not replaced. A catastrophic institutional decline unfolded over roughly eight years without a single visible triggering event.

The numbers are stark once assembled. Between 2010 and 2017, the ranks of revenue agents, the accountants who conduct the substantive audits, fell by about a third. There are now fewer than 10,000 of them, a threshold last crossed in the early 1950s, when the American economy was a fraction of its present size. Another wave is already visible: much of the remaining workforce is at or near retirement eligibility, and the pipeline behind them is thin, because for most of a decade there was almost no hiring at the entry level.

Fewer auditors mean fewer audits. The number of returns examined each year has fallen by hundreds of thousands, a drop of roughly two-fifths in the audit rate, and those that survive are not the examinations they used to be. Auditors describe a working culture reoriented around closing files: rather than following an anomaly wherever it leads, they narrow the scope of a case to the single issue that flagged it, resolve that, and move on, because the caseload behind them never stops arriving. What was once an open-ended inquiry is increasingly a spot check.

The erosion runs deeper than audits. Some of the agency's most basic compliance functions have contracted to the point of near-disappearance. New investigations of people who simply never filed a return at all have fallen by something like 85 percent. And tax debts the agency has already identified and assessed are now expiring in large volumes. The government has ten years to collect an assessed debt before the statute of limitations closes; the value of the debts that lapse each year has climbed from the hundreds of millions of dollars into the billions. This is money the IRS has already found and already established that someone owes. It runs out the clock because nobody is left to chase it.

The decline has not been evenly distributed, and that is the part that matters most. Enforcement against large corporations and wealthy individuals has degraded fastest, because those cases depend on scarce specialist expertise: in transfer pricing, in partnership structures, in the layered vehicles through which serious wealth is held. That expertise takes years to build and leaves in a single afternoon, and it is precisely the expertise most valuable to the private firms that hire departing agents. Meanwhile the great majority of ordinary taxpayers, whose wages and interest are reported to the government by third parties, deal with an automated system that runs more or less as before. The parts of the tax system that require judgment have weakened; the parts that require only matching have not.

One category of enforcement was politically protected and therefore grew as a share of a shrinking total: audits of the earned income tax credit, the refundable credit that supplements the wages of low-paid workers and is the country's largest anti-poverty program delivered through the tax code. Republicans in Congress have pressed hard on its error rate, and the agency responded, in part because those audits are cheap: conducted by mail, largely by machine, requiring no specialist at all. The consequence is a system in which a household claiming the credit, often on an income under $20,000, faces an audit rate comparable to that of someone earning half a million dollars or more.

Our analysis puts the revenue forgone at a minimum of $18 billion a year, and that figure is conservative: it counts only what enforcement at previous levels would have recovered, not the deterrent effect that no longer exists. Former commissioners and senior officials from both parties worry most about that second, unquantifiable piece. A tax system of this scale runs on voluntary compliance, and voluntary compliance runs on the widely held belief that cheating carries a meaningful risk of being caught. That belief is now, in significant measure, inaccurate. If it corrects itself, and word spreads that the odds have changed, the losses will not stay at $18 billion, and the deficit Congress cites when it cuts the agency's budget will grow because it cut the agency's budget.

Out in the Gulf, the foreign companies are still working the shelf.
