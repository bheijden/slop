The linked article does what these articles always do. It gestures at the cost of living, at the general environment for small business in this city, at the difficulty of finding staff, and never once puts the words supply and demand next to each other.

Lay out the arithmetic. Over a few years San Francisco raised the base wage a long way, and not in isolation. Stacked on top: an employer health-spending mandate well beyond what federal law requires, mandatory paid sick leave, paid parental leave. Each is defensible on its own terms and I am not arguing them individually. But together, the true cost of an hour of restaurant labour, not the posted wage but the loaded cost with payroll taxes and mandates and the overhead of complying with all of it, went up by something on the order of half. That is the number that matters to whoever decides how many people work the floor on a Tuesday.

My benchmark, and I do not think it is a controversial one, is that real wages rise sustainably when the value of the work rises. I represented software engineers professionally in the early nineties. The salaries I negotiated then, carried forward for inflation, would not clear six figures today. Engineers of the same individual ability now make several times that, and no ordinance did it. The leverage on an engineer's day went up enormously: the same person's output touches millions of users and millions in revenue in a way it simply did not in 1992, so employers bid against one another for them voluntarily. Nobody in Mountain View is in a conference room working out how to have fewer of them.

Restaurant service is hard work. I did it putting myself through school, and I have never once thought it was overpaid. But the honest question is not whether it is hard or whether the people doing it deserve more, because I think it is and I think they do. The question is whether a server in 2018 produces measurably more value per hour than a server in 2013 doing the identical job in the identical room. As far as I can see, no. The job has not changed. No new tool multiplied it.

When an input gets more expensive because it became more valuable, employers absorb the cost cheerfully, since they get something for it. When the price is raised by decree and the underlying value did not move, the difference comes out of margin, menu prices, hours, or headcount. Restaurant margins are famously thin and menu prices are capped by what customers will actually pay, so it comes out of hours and headcount. That is not a moral claim about anyone. It is a demand curve.

Which brings us to what the article describes without explaining: order at the counter, order on a tablet, order on your own phone, pick it up at the window, bus your own table. The interesting fact is not that self-service exists, since cafeterias and lunch counters are older than everyone in this thread. It is which establishments are adopting it. Places with tablecloths and a wine list, whose entire proposition was that somebody attends to you, are now putting a QR code on the table. Those operators were not hunting for an excuse to cut service. They were pushed into a format they would previously have called a downgrade of their own product.

And in fairness to the other side, that trade may well be worth making. A server who keeps her job at the new wage, with health coverage and sick days she can actually use, is meaningfully better off, and there is a real argument that the aggregate welfare of restaurant workers in this city went up. I am not certain it did not. What I object to is the pretence that the policy is costless, or that whatever cost exists lands on owners. It does not land on owners; owners adjust, and adjusting is what we are watching. It lands on the person who is no longer on the schedule, on the second server who is never hired, on the worker with the thinnest resume and the least English who would have gotten the entry-level job that is now an iPad on a stand. Those people are restaurant workers too. They just do not appear in wage statistics, because you only appear in wage statistics if you have a wage.

If you want to argue that is a price worth paying, argue it. Some days I would agree with you. But write that article, instead of the one that blames the fog and the rent.
