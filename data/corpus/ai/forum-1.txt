I watched one of these from about six feet away, in Northern California in the early nineties, and it permanently changed what I understand the phrase "civil forfeiture" to mean.

I was dating a woman whose family had come up from Mexico two decades earlier and built the kind of small-business ladder people put in speeches: restaurants first, then a bakery, then a couple of other things, and eventually one of her brothers had a chain of used car lots. Nothing glamorous. Buy-here-pay-here inventory, hand-painted prices on the windshields, a guy on staff whose whole job was detailing the cars so they looked good on the lot.

That detailer got caught with a small quantity of marijuana in his own apartment, on his own time. Personal amount. What happened next is the part I still cannot tell people without watching their expression change.

Within about a week the department had a theory. The lots were a front. Cars are how you move value, cash sales are how you launder it, and an employee who handles the inventory and keeps weed at home must be the visible edge of a distribution network. On that theory they took the lots. They took the entire inventory, every vehicle on every lot, including cars customers had already been making payments on. They took his house. They froze the business and personal accounts. The family put the total at around half a million dollars in early-nineties money, essentially everything twenty years of work had produced.

The district attorney read the file and declined to prosecute. Not "declined for now" but declined, and said why: no connection between the employee and the owner beyond a paycheck; a taped statement from the detailer taken under conditions he called coercive and would not put before a jury; no corroborating evidence for any of the rest. He was pointed about the police conduct, which I remember because the family briefly believed it meant the thing was over.

It meant nothing, and this is the part people who have not been near it refuse to believe. The forfeiture case was not against him. It was against the property, captioned as though the land and the cars were the defendants. A decision not to charge a human being has no effect on a proceeding whose defendant is a parcel of real estate. Nobody gave anything back. He had to sue to recover his own property, as a plaintiff, on his own money, with his accounts frozen.

And the department's position, in close to these words, was that seized assets are returned to people acquitted at trial. Follow that through. A man who is never charged is never tried, and a man who is never tried is never acquitted, so under the department's own rule he sits permanently outside the class of people entitled to anything back. Being too plainly innocent to charge left him worse off than being guilty would have.

The doctrine was not really the weapon, though. Attrition was. Continuance after continuance. Demands that he produce professional appraisals of vehicles auctioned off within days of the seizure, which no longer existed anywhere he could reach. Refusal to let him into his own house, where the records documenting the lots' legitimate cash flow sat in a filing cabinet. Hearings the department did not appear for, which cost them a morning and cost him another month of legal fees and another month with no income. None of it is dramatic. It is a slow bleed applied by an institution that does not bleed.

About two years in the offer came: fifty thousand dollars, take it or leave it, no negotiation, against roughly half a million taken. His lawyer told him to take it, and his lawyer was right, because the city could keep doing this as long as it cared to and he could not. He signed.

So be careful with three arguments in particular. That the targets are really dealers and forfeiture is how you reach them when the criminal case is hard to make: here a prosecutor looked at the criminal case and said in public that there was not one. That police would not take from someone they knew to be innocent: they were told there was nothing there, kept the property anyway, and litigated two years to hold it. That an innocent owner can simply get it back: getting it back is a suit you finance yourself against an opponent with public money and unlimited patience, while the assets you would ordinarily use to finance it are the assets in dispute. The process is the penalty, and everyone administering it knows that.

That was more than thirty years ago and nothing since suggests improvement. Equitable sharing made the incentive more direct rather than less, and the roadside variant, where the property is cash in a car and the owner is a thousand miles from home, barely existed then and is routine now. I hope the Court says something useful. I would not plan your life around it.
