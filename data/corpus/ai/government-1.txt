The death of a relative or friend in France brings decisions most people have never had to make before, taken at a distance and inside an unfamiliar legal system. This guidance sets out the main steps in the order they usually arise. Consular staff at the British Embassy in Paris and at consulates elsewhere in France can explain local procedure and put you in touch with translators, lawyers and funeral directors, but they cannot make arrangements on your behalf, and the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office cannot pay any of the costs involved.

Before you commit to anything, find out whether the person who died held travel insurance. It is the first thing to establish, because it determines who pays for everything that follows. A travel policy will often cover the cost of bringing the body or the ashes back to the UK, and may also cover medical treatment given before death, legal fees, interpreting and translation. Insurers who provide it will usually appoint a funeral director in France and a corresponding firm in the UK and manage the arrangements between them, which lifts a considerable burden from the family. If you cannot find a policy, look beyond the obvious: cover is frequently attached to a bank account or a credit card, or provided by an employer under a contract of employment or a business travel scheme. The bank, the card provider or the employer can confirm whether a policy exists and give you the number to call.

If there was no insurance, a relative or a formally appointed representative will need to appoint a funeral director directly and will be responsible for all of the costs. Those costs, covering fees in France, embalming, the coffin, transport and either a local funeral or repatriation, can be substantial, so ask for a written estimate before you agree to anything. Neither the FCDO nor the embassy can meet them or lend money against them. A small number of lawyers and funeral directors occasionally act without charge or at a reduced rate, but this is decided case by case at their own discretion and cannot be relied on or arranged in advance.

A death must be registered in the country where it happens. In France this is done at the mairie, the town hall of the commune where the person died, normally within 24 hours. The declaration is usually made by the next of kin or by the firm of funeral directors acting for the family, who will often deal with it as a matter of course. The certificate you receive, the acte de deces, is issued in French. You can ask for a multilingual version, which is useful outside France. It will not state the cause of death, which is recorded separately as confidential medical data. Ask for several certified copies at the point of registration. You will need them for banks, pension providers, insurers, utilities and the probate registry, and obtaining more later, from the UK, is slower.

There is no need to register the death again in the UK. A certificate issued by the French authorities is generally accepted for UK purposes, including an application for probate or letters of administration. You may also register the death with the UK authorities. Consular registration is optional, but it produces a UK-style death certificate, which some families find easier to use, and a record of the death is passed to the General Register Office within 12 months, where it can be searched later.

Where the cause of death is unknown, or where it appears unnatural, sudden or violent, the French court will order a post-mortem examination. This is a judicial decision: the family's consent is not required, nor can it choose the doctor. The examination is performed by forensic doctors appointed by the court, at an institut medico-legal. Cultural or religious sensitivities about its timing or conduct may not be possible to accommodate. You should also be aware that tissue samples or organs may be retained for further analysis without the family's consent, and without the family being told that this has happened. If this matters to you, raise it with the funeral director and, where possible, the investigating authority as early as you can.

To bring a body home you will need to instruct a UK funeral director who handles international repatriation and who will work alongside the firm in France. Between them they must assemble the required documents: the death certificate issued by the civil registry, a certificate of embalming and permission to transfer the remains out of France. Repatriation is rarely quick, and the timetable depends on whether any investigation remains open.

If the person is cremated in France and you wish to bring the ashes home, you will usually need the death certificate and the cremation certificate, a permit issued by the local authority allowing the ashes to be taken out of the country, and a customs form on arrival in the UK. Airlines set their own rules on carrying ashes, including whether they may travel in the cabin, so check with the carrier before you travel. One consequence deserves thought. If the body is cremated in France, a coroner in England and Wales cannot afterwards hold an inquest. Repatriating the body keeps that possibility open, and allows for a post-mortem in the UK or, if the family wishes to proceed to cremation here, for a coroner's cremation certificate.

Several tasks remain once the funeral or repatriation is settled. Personal effects held by the police will need to be recovered, and expect delay where items are retained as evidence while an investigation continues. You may need an official translator for documents to be used in the UK, and an English-speaking lawyer if there is property in France or legal proceedings to follow. Finally, cancel the passport of the person who died, to guard against identity fraud. If the document is needed for the repatriation itself, time the cancellation for after the body or ashes arrive in the UK.
