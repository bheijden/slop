Discrimination is commonly defined as treating people in comparable situations differently on the basis of at least one ground prohibited by law. That definition is narrower than the everyday use of the word, and the distinction is visible in the data: across successive European surveys, the feeling of having been discriminated against is consistently more widespread than the discrimination that can be measured, documented or proved. The gap between experience and evidence is particularly wide in sport, which is at once one of the most visible arenas of European public life and one of the least systematically monitored for unequal treatment.

Sport's positive contribution is well attested. It reaches marginalised groups that formal social programmes often fail to reach, in part because participation demands no qualification and little paperwork. It also imposes a shared framework of rules applying identically to every participant, one of the few settings in which people of very different backgrounds meet on formally equal terms. That reputation is reflected in public opinion: around two thirds of EU citizens regard sport as a means of combating discrimination.

The same institution, however, ranks and separates as a matter of design. Competitive sport exists to distinguish between participants by performance, and divides them by gender and disability to make competition meaningful. Categorisation that is legitimate on the field of play does not always remain confined to it. The clearest illustration is the position of women in the structures that run sport, which is markedly less equal than participation figures suggest. Women account for a small minority of the members of the International Olympic Committee, for under a fifth of the governing structures of affiliated bodies, for roughly one in seven of the top decision-making posts in national federations, and for approximately a quarter of coaches.

Where discrimination occurs in sport, it tends to attach to characteristics that are immediately visible: skin colour, body shape and gender. Two analytical distinctions are useful. The first is between deliberate discriminatory acts and perceived discrimination, since the two do not necessarily coincide and policy responses to them differ. The second concerns accumulation: disadvantage in sport is frequently the product of several grounds operating together rather than any one of them alone. Economic migrants are the clearest case. Despite a widespread assumption that sport integrates newcomers with particular ease, migrants may simultaneously face language barriers, the cost of club membership and equipment, insecure residence status, unsociable working hours and hostility directed at national or ethnic origin. Each obstacle is modest in isolation; together they can be decisive.

Two grounds receive comparatively little attention in the sports policy debate. The first is religion. Direct discrimination takes the familiar form of harassment by opponents or by teammates, often prompted by visible religious dress or observance. Indirect discrimination is less conspicuous but may affect more people: training schedules and fixture lists set without regard to periods of religious observance, rules governing mixed-gender participation, and sponsors' logos on kit that conflict with religious belief, such as those of alcohol or gambling companies. Because such effects follow from rules that are general in form, they rarely appear in disciplinary records at all.

The second is disability, where the remedies identified are practical rather than declaratory. They include establishing disability-friendly teams, adapting the rules of a sport, adapting equipment and adapting teaching and coaching methods to different needs. They also include opening non-playing roles, so that people who cannot compete in a given discipline may nonetheless participate in it as coaches or referees. The value of that last point is often underestimated: it addresses exclusion from the sporting community as such, rather than only from the field of play.

Statistical evidence specific to sport is scarce, particularly on racism. Most of what is known derives either from general population surveys that were not designed with sport in mind, or from incident monitoring by NGOs and leagues, which records only what is reported. A 2017 survey by the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights found that 3 % of respondents had experienced racist violence and 24 % racist harassment in the preceding twelve months, with high proportions of Jewish respondents reporting fear of antisemitic insult or physical attack. A further survey in 2018 found that nearly two thirds of victims had never reported the incident to any body. Official statistics therefore understate the phenomenon by an unknown but probably substantial margin, and any comparison between countries or seasons should be read with that caveat.

The evidence base is somewhat stronger for sexual orientation and gender identity. A 2018 survey of LGBTQI+ experience in sport found that nine in ten respondents considered homophobia and transphobia to be a problem in sport, and that 82 % had heard homophobic or transphobic language in the preceding twelve months. Almost one in five had avoided taking up a sport they were drawn to. Transgender respondents reported negative incidents disproportionately often.

The abuse recorded ranged from verbal insults through shoving and physical violence to harassment online. Perpetrators were most often fellow participants and teammates rather than spectators or officials, although coaches were more frequently responsible where transgender people were harassed, which is significant given the authority coaches exercise over selection, training and progression. Most victims did not react at the time and very few made a formal report; a substantial number said they did not know where a report could be made. Asked what would improve matters, respondents favoured measures that change the culture of sport over measures that punish individuals within it: more openly gay sports stars, high-profile awareness campaigns, diversity training for those working in sport, and explicitly inclusive policies at club and federation level all ranked above tougher sanctions.

Taken together, the surveys point less to a shortage of rules than to a shortage of visibility, of accessible reporting channels and of data collected in sporting settings themselves. Those are areas in which EU support programmes and coordination between federations can realistically make a difference.
