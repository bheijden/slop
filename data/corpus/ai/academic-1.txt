Diet is routinely nominated as the leading driver of skull diversification in bony fishes, and the reasoning behind that nomination is mechanical rather than merely correlational. Prey items differ in hardness, size, evasiveness and position relative to the substrate, and each of those properties makes a different demand of the apparatus that captures and processes them. A fish that crushes molluscs must generate and withstand large static forces; a fish that pursues other fishes must close its jaws quickly enough to overtake an escape response; a fish that extracts invertebrates from sediment or waterlogged wood must manoeuvre the snout precisely within a cluttered space. Because these demands are at least partly incompatible, with force and velocity trading off across a single lever and skeletal reinforcement seldom coming free, lineages that differ in diet are expected to differ predictably in cranial form. Work on cichlids, labrids, characiforms and centrarchids has broadly borne this out, and the resulting form-diet association is among the more portable generalisations in fish functional morphology.

Two structures carry most of the empirical weight in such studies. The neurocranium integrates the sensory capsules, supplies the origin for much of the adductor musculature and sets the geometry of the suspensorium, so that changes in its proportions propagate directly into how the jaws are loaded and moved. The lower jaw is more tractable still: it can be treated as a lever whose in-lever and out-lever lengths are recoverable from a few homologous points, and whose ratio, mechanical advantage, carries a clear functional interpretation. The empirical direction of that relationship is well established. High closing mechanical advantage, favouring force transmission, accompanies diets of hard, attached or armoured prey; low mechanical advantage, favouring displacement and speed at the jaw tip, accompanies diets of fast or evasive prey. Mechanical advantage is therefore one of the few performance proxies that can be estimated from skeletal material alone and compared across a clade without direct behavioural observation.

That convenience comes with a well-documented complication. Mechanical advantage, like most performance metrics assembled from linked skeletal elements, is computed from more parameters than it has dimensions, so many distinct anatomies yield an identical output. This functional redundancy, or many-to-one mapping of form to function, is conventionally illustrated with the four-bar linkage underlying premaxillary protrusion, in which different combinations of link lengths produce the same kinematic transmission coefficient. Where redundancy is present, morphospace and performance space are not isomorphic: selection may act consistently on performance while shape wanders among equivalent solutions, and the correlation between morphology and diet that a purely shape-based analysis would recover is correspondingly weakened. The methodological implication is that shape and performance should be modelled separately rather than treated as interchangeable, and that the evolutionary question, namely whether performance is drawn towards diet-specific optima, is best asked of the performance estimates themselves.

We ask that question in a clade of weakly electric knifefishes that occupies the deep channels of large lowland Amazonian rivers. These are dark, structurally simple and seasonally hypoxic habitats in which electroreception rather than vision governs foraging, and in which this clade is the numerically dominant group of vertebrates. Its members span a striking range of snout and jaw proportions, from short and blunt to extremely elongate and strongly decurved, and an equally wide range of feeding habits. Some probe sediment and submerged wood for chironomid and other insect larvae; others specialise on parts of other fishes, taking scales and the tips of tails and fins, in several cases from other electric fishes. The clade thus offers, at modest taxonomic scope, both the trophic contrast and the morphological disparity that a form-function analysis requires.

Earlier work on the group has rarely examined form and diet in the same study. Cranial anatomy is described in detail in the systematic literature and diets are known from gut-content surveys, but the two bodies of evidence have been assembled independently and for different purposes, and none of the existing work combines performance estimates with phylogenetic correction. It therefore remains unclear whether the repeated evolution of elongate snouts and low-advantage jaws reflects convergence on diet-associated adaptive peaks, or the accumulation of change largely unconstrained by feeding demand.

Diet in these rivers is also not a fixed attribute of a species. The annual flood cycle alternates between a high-water phase, when fishes disperse into inundated floodplain forest and gain access to terrestrial food, and a low-water phase, when they are confined to the channels themselves, where density rises, dissolved oxygen falls and competition sharpens. Several species respond by switching facultatively to scale-eating during low water, so that trophic position is better described as a distribution across the year than as a single categorical state, and is best estimated from sources with contrasting integration times rather than from stomach contents alone.

Here we combine three-dimensional landmark morphometrics of the neurocranium and lower jaw, lever-model estimates of jaw mechanical advantage, stable isotope estimates of trophic position, gut-content data and phylogenetic comparative model fitting across eleven species. We compare single-optimum, multiple-optimum and neutral-drift models of performance evolution, asking whether jaw performance is pulled towards distinct optima corresponding to diet categories, towards one clade-wide optimum, or is instead consistent with unconstrained divergence along the phylogeny.
