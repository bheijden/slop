There is an hour, known to every man who has been honest with himself for the length of a morning, when the mind is visited although it has sent for nothing. No book lay open at his elbow; no bell had rung; nobody had exhorted him. A judgment arrived, entire and unargued, and while it lasted no doctor of the schools could have argued him out of it. That hour is the whole of religion. The rest is commentary, and commentary, however industrious, is a poor trade for a soul.

The visitation is immediate or it is nothing. Between the mind and that which addresses it there is no standing room for a third person, and where a third has contrived to plant himself he stands in the light. What the teacher offers me is the account of his own morning; he is a window somebody has painted, and had he been clear glass I should have seen the sun and not his colors. The temple is a roof raised over the spot where a man once knelt in the open weather, and the roof is now thought holier than the kneeling. The scripture is the minute of a conversation to which I was not invited, and which I might have held myself had I not been reading. These things are not false; they are at second hand; and the soul, patient in so much else, has this one incorrigible pride, that it will not wear another man's clothes.

Hence I distrust, at the cost of seeming ungracious to much that is venerable, the person who cannot name the divine except in the dialect of one small ancient people who had their own weather and their own way of speaking to the wind. If he has seen something, let him tell me what he saw. If he must travel back some thousands of miles and years to fetch his nouns, if his God will not appear except in the costume of a shepherd's simile, then he is remembering and not perceiving, and I attend to him as I would to any other good memory. The report is worth less than the sight by the whole distance that lies between a map and a county.

I go further. Our reverence for what lies behind us is no harmless piety; it is an injury done daily to the understanding. I would not burn the chronicles; I would put them where they belong, which is the place of illustration. Read the fall of a city as a fable of your own condition, and history becomes a lamp; read it as a decree, and it is a sentence passed upon you before you were born. To be told that the great questions were settled in an age of worse roads and worse lamps than ours is to be told that the sun must not rise because it once rose.

Go out of doors and see how little nature troubles itself with any of this. The pine on the ridge is not an apprentice pine, waiting to be admitted to the guild of pines; it is finished at every hour of its growing, and its whole business is with the light now falling. Man alone lives before and behind himself. He stands always a little to one side of his own life, regretting and expecting, and lets the single moment that was ever his slip by unattended. The animals are at least punctual, and it is the graver charge against us that we are perpetually late for our own existence.

Quotation is the crutch of the mind's boyhood, and no shame in a boy. The lad borrows his father's phrase as he borrows his father's coat, and both, if he grows, are one day given away. But when a man has climbed to the place from which the saying came, and sees for himself the prospect it was made to describe, he finds the words have quietly become unnecessary. He may keep them for their music; he no longer needs them for their meaning. The surest sign that a truth is still another's is the accuracy with which we recite it.

The condition I describe cannot be reached by imitation, for imitation is a confession that we have agreed to be a copy. Nor does it come attended. It brings no gratitude, since gratitude looks over its shoulder for a benefactor. It brings no hope, for hope is the tenant's respect for a landlord who has not yet arrived. It brings not even joy, joy being a receipt for something delivered from outside. It brings instead a great steadiness, which is its own weather and asks nothing of the climate.

This is why the doctrine is resented wherever it is stated. Men will hear that virtue is admirable; they will not hear that virtue exists only in the doing of it. Power does not reside in what a man has done. It exists only in the passage from one condition into another; and the instant he sits down upon his achievement he is as poor as any beggar. Every honor we pay to what is completed is a small vote against what is possible, which is why the world so much prefers to honor the dead.

The teaching goes by the name of self-reliance, and I think the name a weak one. It suggests a man leaning upon himself, which is only the old dependence turned inward. What I mean is not a leaning at all. It is the presence in a person of a principle that neither argues nor leans, and which, being present, is obeyed as the light is obeyed. Such a man does not command, and is not aware of commanding; but rooms alter when he enters them, and his opinions are consulted by people who have never heard him give one. That is not reliance. It is not something he is doing with himself. It is something that has consented, at last, to be done through him.
