To make the preceding argument concrete, we work through a case in which a body of NLP research has already accumulated: work on African-American English. This is one of the few varieties for which bias has been documented repeatedly and across tasks, which makes it a useful test of whether documentation alone is enough.

The empirical picture is consistent. Part-of-speech taggers trained on standard newswire corpora degrade on text carrying features of the variety, and dependency parsers trained on the same resources show comparable drops. Language identification systems misclassify such text, often assigning it to a language other than English, with knock-on effects for any pipeline that filters by predicted language. Toxicity and abusive-language classifiers assign higher scores to text with these features than to matched text without them. The degradation is thus not an artefact of any one task or model family.

What the studies do not share is a notion of what makes this a case of racial bias. Most report a performance gap, comparing system behaviour on text with and without features of the variety, and treat the gap as self-evidently the harm. A minority argue from downstream consequences, and those are worth listing, because they are the closest the literature comes to a normative claim: that systematically worse performance reinforces existing negative perceptions of the variety; that toxicity classifiers cause posts written in it to be removed at disproportionate rates, shrinking the online space in which it can be used at all; and that speakers bear an additional cost when they alter how they write to avoid that outcome. These are harms of a different kind from a gap in tagging accuracy, and they are not derivable from the gap alone.

Even the papers that name those consequences stop short of the relevant scholarship. None of the surveyed work engages with the substantial literature on the variety itself, with work on racial hierarchy, or with work on raciolinguistic ideology. The systems under study are therefore never situated with respect to who actually speaks the variety, or to how those speakers are regarded and treated. Race enters these papers as a property of text, mediated by a feature list or a demographically inferred corpus, rather than as a social structure in which both the speakers and the technologists are positioned.

The omission matters because that literature is not neutral background. It documents a deficit view of the variety as bad English that has survived more than half a century of evidence that it is systematic and rule-governed, with its own phonology, morphology and syntax. That view is not confined to attitudes. It travels with allocational harms: children assessed as language-deficient and routed into remedial tracks on the basis of their home variety; housing seekers screened by voice and told, in numbers that vary by city but not in direction, that the unit has been taken; witnesses whose testimony is transcribed inaccurately by certified court reporters and disbelieved by juries who report that they could not follow what was said. The performance gaps NLP measures sit in that context, not apart from it.

Raciolinguistic ideology names the mechanism that connects them. Racialized communities are constructed as linguistically deficient and therefore as requiring remediation, with the result that access to institutional goods is made conditional on approximating the language practices of a dominant white listening subject. This has a direct implication for how mitigation is framed. When the practical advice available to a speaker is to write differently so that the classifier will not flag them, the system has not reduced harm; it has relocated the burden onto the person harmed and reproduced the ideology that made the deficit view plausible in the first place.

Grounding the work in this literature supplies the normative argument that the surveyed papers lack. It gives a reason why performance gaps matter that does not reduce to engineering shortfall. A tagger that fails on the variety is not merely inaccurate; deployed at scale, it re-stigmatises the variety as the kind of language machines cannot handle, and participates in the same ideological system that licenses discrimination in classrooms, rental markets and courtrooms. That is an argument about legitimacy, and it can be made only from outside the evaluation metric.

It also changes what counts as a solution. If racial hierarchy and language ideology are structural conditions on how systems are conceived, funded, trained and deployed, then measurement and mitigation techniques operating inside the model will remain incomplete however refined they become. Debiasing a representation does not alter who decides which varieties are worth supporting, whose data are collected and on what terms, or who can contest a deployment after the fact. Addressing those conditions requires attending to the power relationship between technologists and the communities their systems act upon, including whether affected speakers have any standing in the design process at all. We take up specific research directions in the following section, noting here that anti-racist language pedagogy, which has spent decades on the problem of working with a stigmatised variety without demanding that its speakers abandon it, is an unusually direct source of guidance for a field that has barely begun to ask the question.
