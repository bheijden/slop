In the Notice we sought comment on whether the predicate findings of the prior Order remain sound, and specifically on whether providers of mass-market retail broadband internet access service, whether delivered over fixed residential facilities or mobile networks, continue to possess both the incentive and the ability to interfere with the open exchange of traffic between their subscribers and the edge providers, applications, services, and devices those subscribers seek to reach. The record assembled in response is extensive, and it convinces us that those findings not only survive but are strengthened by intervening market developments. We therefore reaffirm our earlier determination that last-mile access providers have strong and continuing incentives to restrict openness, and we find, on this refreshed record, that the ability to act on those incentives is undiminished. That determination rests on four mutually reinforcing bodies of evidence: the economic structure of the broadband access market as a two-sided platform terminating in a bottleneck; the documented history of interference by providers subject to our jurisdiction; the persistently weak disciplining effect of consumer switching; and the limited number of competing providers available at the household level, particularly at speeds consumers actually demand.

The economic analysis begins with the observation, uncontested in its essentials by any commenter addressing the point, that a broadband access provider operates a two-sided platform: it sells transmission to end users on one side and, whether or not it charges for it today, controls the only path by which edge providers may reach those end users on the other. Because an edge provider seeking to serve a given subscriber has no alternative route to that subscriber, the access provider occupies what the record and the economic literature describe as a terminating monopoly - a position of unilateral control over the final segment of the path to a particular end user, held irrespective of how many providers compete for that end user's initial subscription. The consequence is that competition on the subscriber-facing side of the platform does not discipline conduct on the edge-facing side, because the provider's leverage over the edge provider persists so long as the provider retains the subscriber. The reviewing court has already accepted this reasoning as "reasonable and grounded in substantial evidence," and has recognized that broadband providers are "capable of acting as gatekeepers" with respect to traffic destined for their subscribers. Several commenters urge that the analysis is inapposite where retail competition is robust [an argument we address below, and which in any event conflates the two sides of the platform], but the record does not disturb the core proposition: a provider that controls termination may extract charges from, degrade, or block edge providers, may favor its own affiliated content or that of paying partners, and may do so profitably even where subscribers would prefer otherwise, because the subscriber cannot readily observe the conduct, cannot attribute degradation to the provider rather than to the edge service, and cannot in most instances respond by switching.

That the incentive is theoretical in no sense follows from the record, which documents repeated instances in which providers have in fact acted upon it. The record before us catalogs the blocking of competing voice-over-IP applications by a fixed provider; the interference with peer-to-peer file-transfer protocols by a large cable operator through the injection of reset packets, undisclosed to affected subscribers; the blocking of tethering applications by mobile providers whose subscribers had purchased plans permitting such use; the restriction of a preinstalled video calling application to subscribers of certain rate plans; and the exclusion, by mobile providers, of competing mobile payment applications from devices sold on their networks. Commenters representing edge providers describe additional conduct not previously the subject of enforcement, including discriminatory treatment at interconnection points that manifests to subscribers as degradation of specific services. We do not rest on any single episode; we find rather that the pattern, spanning fixed and mobile platforms, multiple providers, and more than a decade, corroborates the economic prediction. That some of this conduct was abandoned after public attention or enforcement does not counsel a different conclusion, since the relevant question is whether the incentive exists, not whether every manifestation of it has persisted.

The record likewise forecloses reliance on consumer switching as an adequate constraint. Switching costs in this market are substantial and, in material respects, deliberately constructed: term commitments with early termination liability, equipment leases and return obligations, installation and activation charges, bundled discounts forfeited on departure, and the transaction costs of scheduling service transitions. Record data indicate that annual churn among fixed residential subscribers remains in the range of one to two percent per month for the largest providers, and survey evidence submitted in this proceeding reports that a majority of subscribers dissatisfied with their provider did not switch, most frequently citing the absence of an acceptable alternative. Switching is in any event an implausible remedy for the harms at issue, since the conduct that concerns us is frequently undetectable by the subscriber and, where detected, is often uniform across the available alternatives.

Finally, the availability of alternatives is itself constrained. Record deployment data show that a substantial share of households - the figure exceeds fifty percent at the higher speed tiers on the record - have access to no more than one provider offering service at those speeds, and that a meaningful additional share have access to none. Mobile service, while more competitive at the retail level, is not on this record a substitute for fixed service for most households given usage allowances, latency, and price per unit of data, and mobile providers occupy the identical terminating position with respect to their own subscribers. Commenters asserting that intermodal and satellite entry has rendered these figures obsolete have not supplied data displacing them. We accordingly find, on the whole record, that the incentive and the ability both persist.
