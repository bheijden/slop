The window garden may be a very small affair or a very considerable one. A single jardinière set upon a stand, or half a dozen pots ranged along the sill, will give a good deal of pleasure through the dark months; while at the other extreme one may fit a whole window with a box below, shelves across the glass, and brackets at either side, and fill the whole with bloom. Neither extreme is the mark of success. The costly fitting and the crowded collection are alike beside the point, and the reader will do better with eight plants well housed and well tended than with thirty that cannot be reached to be watered, turned, or washed.

The box itself is easily made by any carpenter, and its length should be that of the window it is to stand in, its width some ten or twelve inches. As to depth, the box may be made six inches deep; but a preferable arrangement is to make it eight, since the additional room allows a shallow tray of zinc to be fitted within it, and above the tray a false bottom of narrow wooden slats, set some two inches clear of the metal. The drainage water then passes through the slats and gathers in the tray, out of sight and off the carpet.

Such a tray should be furnished by the tinsmith with a small tap or plug at the level of its bottom, so that the water standing there may be drawn off every day or two into a basin. This is not a refinement but a necessity; if the water is suffered to rise until it touches the false bottom, the soil above stands wet, the roots sour, and the plants go yellow in spite of every other care. Emptied faithfully, the tray permits the whole box to be watered freely indoors, and this freedom is the entire object of the contrivance.

Since the box stands where every visitor sees it, it may be dressed as handsomely as the room warrants: decorative tiles set in and held by a molding, or a light lattice of wood carried round the sides, are both in good taste. Yet a plain box, stoutly made, with a molding at top and bottom and painted a quiet green or drab, is the sounder choice, for it retires behind the foliage and is not seen at all, which is precisely what is wanted. Brackets, stands, and planters may also be had ready-made of any large florist.

The box is commonly fixed to the window frame, or carried on brackets screwed beneath the sill. A better plan is a low stand of the proper height, light in build and running on small casters. The box may then be drawn back from the glass on a bitter night, turned end for end every week or two so that each side takes its share of the light, and swung about to present its best face to the room. To this may be added swinging brackets, one or two pots on either side of the window, with a third set halfway up the frame; these should be hinged at the base so that they may be swung in or out at pleasure. Bracket plants dry out faster than any others and that this is the most difficult position in the window to manage well.

For exposure, a south, southeast, or east window is to be chosen. Winter light is a scanty commodity, and plants that are expected to flower, rather than merely to keep their leaves, need every hour of sun the season affords. The window should be tight and free from draughts about the sashes, and inside shutters, with a curtain drawn across on cold nights, will save many a plant.

Steadiness of temperature is of more consequence than the actual figure. A plant that is comfortable at night and near the freezing point before morning suffers severely, and often dies of it; some kinds endure such usage, but none prosper under it. Where the rooms are heated with steam, hot water, or hot air, the opposite fault is quite as common, and overheating will ruin a collection as surely as chilling. It is worth observing that a house of brick or stone, once closed for the night, holds its warmth till morning except in the extremest weather, whereas a lightly built wooden house, standing exposed on all sides, parts with its heat very quickly.

Rooms lighted by gas are difficult to grow anything in, and the air of an ordinary living room is in any case far too dry for foliage. Where there is a bow-window it may be shut off from the room by glass doors, and one then has a little conservatory of a very serviceable kind. Failing that, a pan of water kept on the stove or over the register, and damp moss tucked in among the pots, will do a good deal to temper the atmosphere.

The foliage must be washed from time to time, for house dust settles thickly and stops the leaves from doing their work. A bathtub that drains readily is the best place. Each plant is laid on its side, propped on a small box so that the pot is clear of the bottom, and syringed thoroughly, the soil thus spared a soaking. Water should be kept from the blooms, and particularly from thick white waxy flowers such as the hyacinth, which spot and go brown. Begonias and others of velvety leaf are better wiped with cotton, dry or barely damp; though even these may be syringed if the leaves can be dried quickly out of doors on a mild day, or near the stove.

The plants may be turned out and planted directly in soil in the box, but they are better kept in their pots, since they can then be rearranged at will and the backward ones brought forward into the light. Porous clay pots suit the larger specimens on the shelves and brackets. For the small plants that fill the box, the light flexible paper pots are excellent, as they may be crowded close together and waste no room. Between pots that stand apart, damp sphagnum or other moss should be packed; it steadies them, keeps the soil from drying, and gives off a moisture the foliage is grateful for.

Florists keep plants raised expressly for window culture and winter bloom, and a good one will take pleasure in making up a suitable collection if the order is given in early autumn, before the season's press of business. Most of the subjects fit for the purpose come from the medium house, where the night temperature is about 60 degrees, or the cool house, at about 50, the day running ten to fifteen degrees warmer than the night. Five degrees below these figures does no harm; more is borne, but growth is checked by it. A higher day temperature may be allowed in bright sun than in dull weather.
