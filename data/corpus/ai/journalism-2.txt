The Scottish National Portrait Gallery in Edinburgh has reopened to the public after a closure of two and a half years and a refurbishment costing £17.6 million, or about US$27.4 million at current rates. Wikinews attended a press tour of the building ahead of the reopening, during which staff showed journalists the restored Victorian interiors, the newly opened galleries and the changes made to get visitors into and around a building that had become difficult for many of them to use.

The most immediate result of the work is space. By converting areas that had been given over to offices, workshops and storage, and by returning parts of the building to their original purpose, the gallery has increased its exhibition space by roughly 60 per cent. It can now show seventeen exhibitions at the same time, and a far larger proportion of a collection that had, for decades, been mostly out of sight. Staff said the institution holds many thousands of portraits and photographs and had been able to display only a fraction of them at once.

The building itself is among the more distinctive in Edinburgh's New Town: a dark red sandstone block in the Gothic revival manner, designed by the architect Robert Rowand Anderson and opened in 1889. It was paid for by John Ritchie Findlay, the proprietor of The Scotsman newspaper and a philanthropist, who met the construction costs and gave the building to the nation. It was conceived as the world's first purpose-built portrait gallery. For much of its early life it was shared: the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland and its collection of national antiquities occupied part of the building until the mid-1950s, an arrangement that helps explain the warren of rooms and partitions the refurbishment has now undone.

Much of the money and design effort went on getting people through the doors. The previous lift held two people at a time and was, in the words of one member of staff, hopeless for wheelchair users, families with pushchairs or anyone carrying anything. It has been replaced by a large glass lift serving all floors, positioned so that visitors using it move through the building on much the same route as everyone else rather than being sent round the back. At the entrances, gently sloped approaches have been built alongside the original stone steps, replacing a wooden ramp that previously had to be requested and put in place by staff. Doorways have been widened and thresholds levelled throughout.

Admission remains free, as it is at Scotland's other national collections, and the gallery expects the combination of free entry, more space and better access to roughly double its annual attendance to somewhere near 300,000 people a year. Planning has been coordinated with the National Museum of Scotland, a short walk away on Chambers Street, which reopened earlier this year after a refurbishment of its own. Staff said the two institutions had compared notes on practical matters as well as programming: where pushchairs can be parked, where baby-changing facilities are located, how families move between the two buildings in the course of a day. The intention, one curator said, is that a visitor should not have to plan a visit as a logistical exercise.

The ground floor has been given over in large part to contemporary material. One gallery is devoted to photography and will rotate its contents regularly; the opening display is a wall of portraits of Scottish public figures from the present day, among them actors familiar from British television drama, sportspeople, writers and musicians. It is intended as an accessible point of entry for visitors who may not arrive with a strong interest in seventeenth-century portraiture, and as a reminder that the gallery continues to commission and collect.

Nearby, in a darkened room, is a video installation on the subject of missing persons. The work grew out of a theatre production and incorporates footage shot in some of Edinburgh's more deprived housing areas, a long way in every sense from the New Town streets outside. It sits deliberately close to the entrance, and the contrast between the two ground-floor displays, celebrity portraiture on one side and disappearance on the other, is one the curators appear to have intended.

Upstairs, the changes are subtler but more extensive. False ceilings installed in the twentieth century have been stripped out, restoring the original proportions of the top-floor galleries, and skylights that had been covered over or removed have been recreated, so that the rooms are once again lit from above as Anderson designed them. Adjustable blinds allow staff to control how much daylight reaches the works, a necessary compromise between the pleasure of natural light and the conservation requirements of paintings and works on paper. Where new stonework was needed, it was cut and finished to match the Victorian fabric; on the tour it was, in most places, impossible for a visitor to tell which was which without being told.

The first of the new thematic displays on the upper floor is devoted to the Scottish Enlightenment of the eighteenth century, the period of David Hume, Adam Smith and their circle, and combines works from the permanent collection with more than a hundred items borrowed for the occasion. The loans include portraits, objects and documents that have not previously been shown together in this context. They hang above parquet floors that were found beneath fitted carpet during the works and have been repaired and restored, one of a number of original features that came to light once the later additions were taken out.

The gallery will now operate as three floors with distinct characters: contemporary photography and new commissions at ground level, thematic historical displays above, and rotating exhibitions between them. Staff said the intention throughout had been to make the building do what it was built to do, and to make it possible for far more people to walk in and use it. On the evidence of the tour, both aims appear to have been met.
