People who have never gone far on their own legs suppose that a walking tour is made for the sake of the views, and will ask you afterwards, with a kind and vacant interest, whether the scenery was fine. It was; but nobody walks a fortnight for scenery. The real matter of a walking tour is not what you see but the temper you are in while seeing it, and that temper is not one thing but a series, changing hourly, like light on a hillside when the clouds are moving.

The morning temper is the boldest and the least valuable. You come out of the inn at eight with the road wet and shining, and your legs are so plainly delighted to be used that you feel a person of enormous capacity. You compose speeches; you settle questions that have puzzled better heads; you overtake a carter and pity him for being obliged to sit down. It is a fine state, and quite unreal. By noon the shine has gone off the day, and by four o'clock you have passed into the other country altogether.

I like the afternoon temper better, though I own it is not so brilliant. Somewhere in the middle of a long day the mind gives up its ambitions and consents to be nothing but a companion to the body. You are not thinking; you are hardly even looking; you are going forward through a mild and continuous dream in which hedges, a dog barking two fields away and the smell of cut grass arrive without being asked for and are none of them examined. Nothing is bright any more, but nothing hurts. The chief pleasure has become an animal one, the plain satisfaction of drawing a large breath and putting one foot in front of the other, and if you were asked what you had been thinking of this last hour you would confess, without embarrassment, that you had not been thinking at all.

The halts are half the tour. It is a great thing to come to a bank under two or three trees and sit down simply because the bank is there, and get out your pipe, and stay. The birds regard you at first as an intrusion and then as furniture; a robin will come and look at you with that unblinking impudence which is the nearest thing in nature to a personal remark. Nobody knows where you are, or would be disappointed if you never got up again. Half the sweetness of the halt is knowing that you might stay there till the shadows are long.

For the great luxury of the road is not exercise or air or scenery, but the loss of the clock. In common life we are tethered to a dial; we lunch because it is one and are bored because it is only three. On the road the hours run together and are astonishingly large. I once spent a week at a farm at the head of a Welsh valley where the almanac had no authority whatever. Sunday was known, because of the bell; the rest of the week was a rumour. I asked the good woman what day of the month it was; she consulted her husband, he consulted the weather, and between them they arrived at something a fortnight out, and were not in the least disturbed. Their days were not short; they were long as childhood is long, and for the same reason, that nobody was counting them.

Yet it is only fair to add that the tether is inside us, and not on the doorpost. A man of business does not carry his hurry because the office insists on it, but because hurry has become the shape of his mind. Put him down in Paradise and he will have made an appointment before dinner, and by the second week be pointing out that something might be organized. There is no cure for such a man on this side of the grave; but there is a palliative, and it is a walking tour, for after four days the note-book grows damp, the pencil disappears, and he finds he has begun to date events, in his own head, by the day it rained.

Then there is the evening, the reward of the whole business, which no idle man ever gets. A long march sharpens every dull pleasure that is waiting at the end of it. Tobacco after twenty miles has a flavour that no tobacco ever had in a drawing-room; a glass of ale becomes a serious, almost a moving experience; and as for reading, a book taken up by candle-light in a bar parlour, with your boots off and your legs pleasantly stiff, comes to you with a sort of violence. Every page seems written for that hour; you half suspect that you wrote it yourself and forgot. Choose your pocket volume with care. Take an essayist you can dip into anywhere, Lamb or Hazlitt for choice; take a small book of songs, since lyric poetry is the only poetry that will stand being read out of doors and in fragments; and take Tristram Shandy, which is the perfect travelling novel, because it has no more idea than you have of where it is going, and does not care.

And before the candle, before the book, there is the loitering at the door of the inn while the sun goes down behind the ricks. The small stiffnesses of your usual character, your notions about company and about your own consequence, have been walked clean out of you, and you will talk to absolutely anybody: to the ostler, to a man with a ferret in his pocket, to two old fellows disputing about a horse that died before you were born. You listen as an audience listens at a good play, wanting nothing, contradicting nobody, admiring the performers. It is the humblest state of mind available to a proud man, and I have never known it survive the journey home.
