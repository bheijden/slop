The words this page needs

Six terms, all of them ours, none of them standard. Nothing below means anything without them.

Signed
A pull request description ending in a line the tool wrote about itself — Generated with…, Co-authored-by…, or an HTML comment holding a shouted sentinel. The footer is cut off before any word is counted, so nothing here can learn the footer itself. About one description in eight carries one.
Unsigned
Every other description. Not a human control. Plenty are machine-written with the footer removed or never added, so the two sides are more alike than they look and every number on this page is a floor rather than a ceiling.
Register
A way of writing, as opposed to a thing written about. Academic, tabloid, legal, chatty are registers; so is the way a machine writes a pull request description. Two authors covering the same lockfile bump in different registers produce different sentences.
Group
A pile of descriptions that use similar words, found by clustering and nothing else — the signatures are hidden while it runs. The corpus is cut into five. One of them is the register, and it is the only one published.
Small group
The same descriptions cut a second time, into ten. These are never published. They exist only to throw words away: the register spreads across several of them, and a word that only ever turns up in one is a word from that corner rather than from the register.
Lift
How many times more often a group uses a word than the whole rest of the corpus does. Drawn at 7.4× means descriptions in that group write it 7.4 times as often as everyone else. This is the number the list is ranked by.

What the signature does and does not say

A signed description was written by a machine. That is the whole of it. It does not say the unsigned ones were written by people — plenty are machine-written with the footer stripped — and it does not say a group holding more signatures is more machine-sounding. Ranking words by how much more the signed half uses them finds the work rather than the writing, and puts a tool's own name at the top.

      Step one — which group is the register

      Every description clustered by word use alone, coarsely, with the signatures hidden from the fit. The group holding the largest share of signed descriptions is the one published. Coarse on purpose: split any finer and the register falls into two groups, and the wrong half wins the signed share. A second, independent test — does this group's vocabulary mark machine writing among descriptions that are not in it — has to agree, and the page says so when it does not.

      The signed share is measured on the last forty days, but the groups can be applied backwards: every description ever sampled is assigned to its nearest one — assigned, not re-fitted, so the groups do not move underneath the chart. What that shows is a second, entirely independent reason to believe the choice. The group picked for having the most signatures is also the one that arrived.

      Step three — what only lives in one corner

      The same descriptions are cut a second time, into ten smaller groups. The register does not turn into one of them — its descriptions scatter, most into one small group and the rest across three or four others. Each word the ranking put forward is then checked inside every small group. A word common enough to be measured in exactly one of them only ever turns up in one corner of the register, so it belongs to that corner rather than to the register, and is dropped however high it ranked. A word too rare to be measured anywhere is kept: this test may remove evidence, never demand it.

      Neither obvious reading of this is right. It is not a rarity filter — the words it drops are rarer in ordinary human prose than the ones it keeps. And it is not merely reaching further down the ranking: on the back-testing corpus a filtered list of 1,200 words catches 21 of 24 machine-written documents against 20 for an unfiltered list of the same length, and 21 against 17 when no false alarm is allowed at all.

          How this is built

          1. Sample. Ten five-minute windows a day, one from each 2.4 hour block, start times drawn from the date so a run can be repeated to the second. About a thousand public pull request descriptions a day.
          2. Split off the signature. Tools sign their work, either as a trailing line that reads like attribution or as a shouted HTML comment. Both are matched on form rather than on product name, and the block is cut from the text before a word is counted, so nothing here can learn the signature itself.
          3. Keep the bag, drop the prose. A description becomes a list of words and how often each occurred. Order is gone, the signature is gone, and the account becomes a number that means nothing outside its own day.
          4. Find the register. Cluster coarsely; take the group with the largest signed share. This is the only place a signature is used, and it is used to pick one group out of five, not to score anything.
          5. Rank against the rest. A word's score is its rate inside that group over its rate in every other description. The second term is what penalises words everybody uses, and it is the whole reason this ranks amendment over here.
          6. Drop what only lives in one corner. Cluster again, finely, and remove any word that is common enough to measure inside a single small group and nowhere else. Words too rare to measure anywhere are kept — demanding evidence rather than merely not-failing costs ten of the twenty-four documents the list catches, because the demand is a frequency floor and the rare words are the signal.

          The unsigned side is not a human control: plenty of machine-written descriptions carry no signature, which makes the two sides more alike, so every number here is a floor.

          Sampled every morning and re-derived on Mondays by GitHub Actions. The sample is in data/docs, one file a day; the fit is in data/cluster.json. Whether any of this carries over to prose that is not about software is measured against data/corpus, twenty-four topics each written once by a person and once by a machine.
